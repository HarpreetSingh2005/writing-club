### SOURCE AUTHORITY
- The RAW TRANSCRIPT is the ultimate authority. The Summary is a preservation aid and may compress; check every flow beat against the transcript itself.
- If the flow and the transcript conflict, FOLLOW THE TRANSCRIPT. Never use the flow to justify a fact that is not supported by the transcript.

### SOURCE-FIDELITY CHECKS (each is a serious failure — reject when present)
A. WRONG / RELABELLED PEOPLE — a person in the flow is renamed, merged, replaced, or reinterpreted (e.g. "small girl in a wheelchair" → "a child", or the person accompanying her → "an uncle").
B. INVENTED EVENTS / ACTIONS — the flow adds an action, event, or sequence not present in the transcript.
C. INVENTED OBSERVATIONS — the flow reports the speaker noticed something the transcript does not contain.
D. INVENTED ATMOSPHERE / SENSORY DETAILS — lighting, sounds, smells, weather, colors, indoor/outdoor, facial expressions, body language, or crowd reactions not in the transcript.
E. INVENTED EMOTIONAL STATES — the speaker or another person is assigned a feeling the transcript does not state.
F. UNSUPPORTED CAUSAL CLAIMS — the flow says one thing caused another when the transcript does not.
G. UNSUPPORTED INTERPRETATION OF THE SPEAKER'S REALIZATION — the realization is replaced, softened, or upgraded into a "cleaner" philosophical point.
H. GENERIC MORAL / MOTIVATIONAL TRANSFORMATION — the flow bends a specific experience into a universal lesson or inspirational conclusion.
I. TOPIC / TITLE MISTAKEN FOR THESIS — a topic label or working title is treated as the article's conclusion.

Any concrete unsupported claim above is a serious failure and requires rejection.
Do NOT suggest adding sensory or atmospheric details to the outline unless they exist in the raw transcript. If the source contains sensory detail, preserve it. If the source does not contain it, do not request or invent it.

Criteria:
1. Does the flow have a clear, non-generic angle?
2. Are the sections logically sequenced to build a strong argument?
3. Does it feel like a human writer structured it?
4. Does it preserve the cleaned summary, including any "Do not lose" points, instead of drifting into generic advice?
5. Does the flow's tone/emotional posture emerge from the source's thought rather than being imposed as a generic label? Does it respect the target word count?
6. Author Skill Fit: evaluate the outline against the "author_skill" block inside the Style Profile (the stable Author Skill's selected tendencies for this article). Specifically:
   - Is the lens shift earned, or is it a manufactured contrarian angle with no supporting reasoning?
   - Is the concrete starting point strong enough to carry observation → deeper meaning?
   - Does the progression from observation to deeper meaning feel natural, or is it forced?
   - Are reveals appropriately timed (not announced at the start, not artificially withheld from the reader)?
   - Is the structure generic (safe, interchangeable) or does it allow the author's natural pacing?
   - Has an unnecessary framework or stylistic device (numbered system, rhetorical questions, pop-culture conceit) been inserted just to look like the author?
   - If the flow's tone label suggests humor or seriousness: does the structure allow humor and seriousness to coexist within the same observation, or does it force an alternating serious-paragraph/joke-paragraph pattern?
7. Source grounding of the outline: Are the sections anchored to actual source observations and specific insights (the incident, the turning point, the concrete details), or do they use generic abstract placeholder phrases such as "personal growth", "resilience", "shared human experience", "finding strength", "unexpected lessons", "broader implications", "social dynamics", "raw truth about motivations", or "broader themes at play"? The outline should preserve the thought progression, not pre-write generic conclusions.
8. If the outline is technically clean but does not reflect the author's way of developing ideas, treat it as a failure of criterion 3 (human structure).
9. Atmosphere / detail invention in the outline: Does the outline introduce visual, sensory, or environmental details that are NOT in the source (lighting, sounds, smells, weather, colors, indoor/outdoor, invented objects, invented people, invented crowd reactions, invented facial expressions)? The outline is a reasoning map, not a screenplay. "Old equipment" must stay "old equipment" — it must not become "rusted equipment, dim lighting, clinking weights, stale air." Reject any beat that upgrades a detail beyond the source.
10. Title/angle pre-solving: Does the title_direction or core_argument announce a generic thesis the article is meant to prove ("Finding Motivation in Unlikely Places", "The speaker discovers motivation can emerge from unexpected places"), instead of naming the actual central tension/observation from the source? If the title/argument could float onto any inspirational article, it is a failure.
11. If revision feedback is provided, does this flow directly address the specific gaps the user pointed out? A revised flow that ignores the user's feedback fails even if it is otherwise clean.

If there are issues, reject the flow and explain exactly what needs to be changed in the sections, emotional posture, or argument.
