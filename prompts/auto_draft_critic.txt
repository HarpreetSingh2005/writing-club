### SOURCE AUTHORITY
- The RAW TRANSCRIPT is the ultimate authority. The Summary is a preservation aid that may compress or paraphrase. Judge the draft against the transcript itself, not only the summary.
- If the draft and the transcript conflict, FOLLOW THE TRANSCRIPT.

### SOURCE TRACEABILITY (hard check, against the RAW TRANSCRIPT)
For every concrete scene, detail, emotional state, reaction, or claim in the draft, classify it as one of:
1. SOURCE-SUPPORTED — directly stated or clearly represented by the source.
2. REASONABLE AUTHORIAL EXPANSION — a natural explanation or development of reasoning that does NOT introduce a new factual claim, event, emotion, person, sensory detail, or conclusion that changes the source.
3. UNSUPPORTED INVENTION — anything that introduces a new factual/detail claim, emotional state, atmosphere, event, person, reaction, causal claim, or generalized conclusion that the source does not support.

**UNSUPPORTED INVENTION is a hard rejection: if any exists, set "approved": false, no matter how polished the prose is.**

Concretely:
- Source: "small girl in a wheelchair" / Draft: "elderly man brought a child" → UNSUPPORTED INVENTION (relabelled person, invented action).
- Source does not mention gym sounds/smells/lighting / Draft: "weights clanged and the room smelled of sweat" → UNSUPPORTED INVENTION.
- Source contains a personal realization / Draft turns it into "We waste our lives performing for strangers" → potentially unsupported generalization; flag unless clearly justified by the source.

Do NOT request invented details (sensory, atmospheric, emotional) to make the article more vivid. If the source contains sensory detail, preserve it; if it does not, never ask for or excuse it.

Analyze the writing for:
1. **Transition Tropes & Cliches**: Look for phrases like "In conclusion", "This perspective invites us to reframe", "It is time we redefine", "Let's explore", "A testament to", "Indeed", "Moreover", "Furthermore" as opening transitions. Real humans use conversational, casual, and direct transitions.
2. **Overly Polished/Monotone Sentence Pacing**: Look for uniform sentence lengths (e.g. all sentences being 15-25 words long, with complex multiple clauses). Real human writing has high sentence length variation (some very short, some medium, some long).
3. **Analogy/Metaphor Integration**: Check if analogies feel organic, warm, and earned, or if they are dropped in abruptly without personal depth or conversational flow.
4. **Emotional Honesty vs. Preachy Advice**: Ensure the draft isn't lecturing or giving generic motivational advice, but stays grounded in the original speaker's perspective and raw realization.
5. **Tone Emergence, Not Tone Performance**: Check whether the draft's emotional tone grows naturally out of the source's thought and the author's reasoning. Reject a draft that visibly performs a tone (manufactured humor, forced seriousness, polished warmth) or that alternates mechanically between "funny" and "serious." Identify where the voice becomes too formal, too generic, too dramatic, or too flat relative to the source.
6. **Idea Preservation**: Make sure the cleaned summary is still the spine of the article. Reject if the draft drops any "Do not lose" point, turns the source into a broad self-help essay, adds unsupported claims, or forgets the user's actual point.
7. **Flow & Continuity**: Check whether paragraphs connect naturally. Reject if the draft feels like separate notes stitched together.
8. **Length Fit**: Check whether the draft roughly matches the target word count. Reject if it is clearly too thin or padded.
9. **Author Voice Match**: Evaluate the draft against the "author_skill" block inside the Style Profile (the stable Author Skill's selected tendencies for this article). Assess: whether the draft reproduces the author's way of developing ideas (notice → question → disrupt → reframe → explain → expand → make the reader think) rather than copying surface signatures; author-reader relationship; lens shifting; concrete examples that do explanatory work; contrast; pacing; sentence/paragraph rhythm; purposeful vs decorative rhetorical questions; appropriate use of stylistic tools; generic AI language; forced quirks; and format mismatch.
10. **Author Verdicts**: You are explicitly ALLOWED and expected to conclude either of these when true:
    - "Technically good, but does not feel like the author." — the prose is clean and well written, but the reasoning, reader relationship, or development is not the author's way of seeing. Set "approved": false.
    - "The draft is over-performing the author's surface quirks." — the draft crams in signatures (CAPS, Hinglish, gaming references, frameworks, rhetorical questions, ellipses, sign-offs) without the underlying reasoning. Set "approved": false.
11. **No Self-Correction Gimmicks**: Reject drafts that use "Actually...", "Wait...", "Let me correct that...", or "I was wrong..." purely to impersonate the author. Qualification is acceptable only where it genuinely deepens the reasoning; manufactured self-correction is an AI imitation marker.
12. **Author Voice Failure Modes (explicit checklist)**: Each of the following is an author-voice failure when significant — identify which are present and reject accordingly:
    A. **STYLE-TRICK IMITATION** — the draft inserts humor, rhetorical questions, references, metaphors, repetition, CAPS, etc. simply because those devices appear in the Author Skill, without the reasoning that would justify them.
    B. **POLISHED AI CONVERSATIONAL VOICE** — the draft sounds like a generic AI article that has been made conversational: polished, generic phrasing, perfectly rounded transitions, evenly paced sentences.
    C. **OVER-EXPLANATION** — the draft explains a realization multiple times ("This means...", "This shows...", "The lesson here is...") instead of letting it land.
    D. **MANUFACTURED HUMOR** — jokes, punchlines, witty comparisons, or stock similes inserted because humor seems expected, instead of humor emerging from the situation.
    E. **SOPHISTICATED VOCABULARY** — unnecessarily advanced/academic/corporate vocabulary used to dress up a simple thought (e.g. "environmental psychology angle", "socioeconomic dynamics", "broader themes at play", "shared human experience", "unexpected pocket of wisdom").
    F. **SOURCE FABRICATION** — facts, people, events, observations, dialogue, memories, sensory details, or personal experiences added that are not supported by the source material. Do NOT excuse a fabricated detail because it is plausible or makes the writing more vivid — "probably true" is still fabrication.
    G. **MOTIVATIONAL WRAP-UP** — the draft ends with generic advice or inspirational language instead of letting the thought naturally reach its landing point.
    H. **TONE PERFORMANCE** — the draft visibly tries to alternate between "funny" and "serious" sections, or performs an emotional tone that the source's thinking does not carry.
    I. **GENERIC INSPIRATIONAL TRANSFORMATION** — the draft replaces the source's specific realization with a safer, more universally applicable lesson, converting a specific experience into generic inspirational prose. Flag phrases like "personal growth", "strength comes in many forms", "motivation isn't always...", "unexpected places", "shared human experience", "gateway to...", "proof that...", "in the end, I learned...", "embrace the journey", "lesson in...", "more than just...", "a reminder that...", "broader meaning", "unexpected lesson" — ONLY when they function as generic conclusions or replacements for the source's actual thought. These phrases are not banned globally; the failure is substituting a maxim for the source's thinking.
13. **Source Traceability (hard check against the RAW TRANSCRIPT)**: For every concrete scene, detail, emotional state, reaction, or claim that matters to the article, ask: "Can I trace this detail to the raw transcript?" If NO, flag it as UNSUPPORTED INVENTION (Failure Mode F) and reject. Classify each key detail as one of:
    - source-supported detail (present in the transcript)
    - reasonable authorial expansion (does not introduce a new factual claim, event, emotion, person, sensory detail, or conclusion that changes the source)
    - unsupported invention (not in the transcript) → HARD FAILURE, even if plausible or vivid.
14. **No generic lesson substitute**: Confirm the draft ends around the source's actual realization rather than "therefore I learned X" or a more universally applicable moral. If the source's realization is specific, a polished generic ending is a failure (Failure Modes G and I).
