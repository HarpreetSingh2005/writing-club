### Lessons from an Unlikely Gym: The Illusion of Being Watched

Walking into that local public gym felt a bit like stepping into an alternate universe. It was a far cry from my usual spot, where the energy buzzed with young bodies and the clanking of weights. Here, the patrons were mostly older folks. Uncles, aunties, and a handful of grandpas shuffled about, doing their thing while I stood awkwardly in the corner. The equipment looked like it had seen better days—rusty weights and machines that creaked like they were protesting against the very act of being used.

I felt out of place. I kept glancing around, half-expecting someone to notice me. Would they raise their eyebrows at my presence? Would they snicker or share a knowing glance? The thought of being judged made my heart race. No one wants to be "that person" in a gym filled with seasoned gym-goers who seemed to have their routines down to an art.

But here's the thing: in the middle of my self-conscious spiraling, an uncle walked in pushing a small child in a wheelchair. The contrast was stark. There I was, worrying about lifting weights while he was navigating a far more challenging reality. As I watched them, I realized something profound: nobody in the gym cared about my workout. They were completely absorbed in their own lives, their own struggles, their own routines. The little girl in the wheelchair, with her bright eyes and infectious smile, was the center of the universe for that uncle. Yet, to everyone else around us, she was just another person. 

This hit me like a ton of bricks. I started to reflect: why was I so concerned about what others thought? If I were to stop working out today, if my body were to fail me in the future, those eyes would still be glued to their own lives. The reality is, people might notice for a moment, maybe gossip or joke about it, but in the end, they move on quickly. They forget. They have their own issues to deal with.

Suddenly, the weight of my anxiety felt lighter. I was reminded of how easily we spiral into self-doubt. Social psychology has a fancy term for it—the spotlight effect. It suggests we overestimate how much others notice us. It turns out, they’re often more wrapped up in their own dramas to pay attention to ours. 

I thought of the uncle and his child again. Empathy can shift the lens of self-consciousness. It’s not all about us. We’re all fighting our own battles, even if they look different on the surface. The push to seek validation from strangers? Completely futile. My health, my well-being, they’re my responsibility. Not dictated by the imagined gazes of gym-goers.

So, why should I care what others think? I realized the answer was simple: I shouldn't. Working out isn’t about impressing anyone; it’s about taking care of myself. I deserve to be in that gym, just as much as they do. 

Looking back now, it seems almost silly that I let myself get caught up in that cycle of worry. The gym isn’t a stage for judgment; it’s a space for growth. I can lift weights, run that mile, and sweat it out—without the fear of scrutiny. After all, we’re all just trying to navigate through life, doing our best with what we have. And maybe, just maybe, the best thing we can do is focus on our own paths rather than the imagined opinions of others.

## 📝 INTAKE CLEANED SUMMARY
----------------------------------------
This is about the idea of why we shouldn't care so much about what other people think. It happened during a time when I really wanted to exercise, but I couldn't go to my regular gym. So, I started going to a local public gym instead—the kind of place filled almost entirely with older uncles and aunties, with no one my age around. The equipment wasn't great, and honestly, it wasn't a motivating environment at all. I really didn't want to work out there because it was far from ideal, and I was sitting there feeling self-conscious, worrying that these people might judge me or make fun of me for exercising there.

Right in the middle of those thoughts, I saw an uncle walk in with a small child in a wheelchair who couldn't walk. As I observed them, I realized something striking: nobody in the park was even looking at her or paying attention. Nobody there cared or gave a second thought to what she was going through.

That moment hit me hard and made me re-evaluate everything. I asked myself, who am I even trying to hide from or showcase my workout to? If I stop working out and my body or legs stop functioning properly in the future, none of these people are going to come support me or take care of me. People might make fun of you for a couple of minutes, but after that, they completely forget you exist. They don't actually care whether you're doing well or not. So why should I care what they think? You just shouldn't care at all.
